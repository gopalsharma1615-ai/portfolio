                                     INTRODUCTION

Quantum field theory provides a framework for the description of three fundamental interactions
(strong, weak and electromagnetic), phase transitions in particle physics and condensed-matter
physics. In which Quantum Electrodynamics is first theory which was successful in describing
the behaviour of subatomic particles which also describes the interaction of light with matter in a
better way. This theory deals with creating and annihilating the particles which is very essential
for the description of subatomic particles. Richard Feynman used the pictorial tools to express
the mathematics of different interaction to calculate their probability amplitude called Feynman
diagrams and used it in solving the problems of quantum electrodynamics which actually
produced the most precise results. In QED theory renormalization is required to treat infinities
which appear in calculated quantities like mass and charge of electron.Renormalization is a
procedure to adjust the theoretical parameters describing a system in such a way as not to change
it’s measurable properties.

     1. FEYNMAN DIAGRAMS
In theoretical physics, Feynman diagrams are pictorial representations of the mathematical
expressions describing the behavior of subatomic particles. The scheme is named after its
inventor, American physicist Richard Feynman, and was first introduced in 1948. The interaction
of sub-atomic particles can be complex and difficult to understand intuitively. Feynman
diagrams give a simple visualization of what would otherwise be an arcane and abstract formula.
[Ref. 4]

The miracle of Feynman diagrams is that a simple set of rules allows us to easily find all of the
important interactions. The first and most predictable powerful quantum field theory QED talks
about the interaction of the electron field with the electromagnetic field. That means interactions
between electrons their antimatter counterparts (the positron), and photons. In Feynman
diagrams, we depict the electron as an Arrow pointing forwards in time or the positive point is an
arrow pointing backwards in time. The photon is shown as a wavy line time, direction is
irrelevant for the photon. Feynman plot his diagrams in space versus time. [Ref. 1]

These are the possible ways that the electromagnetic and electron fields can interact. Every single
QED interaction is built using these interactions, but why only this interaction? Because these follow
some conservation laws.

Energy and momentum conservation requires that particles not to vanish or appear from nothing,
which guarantees that if something goes in and something else must come out. Other one is charge
must also be conserved, if one electron or a positron goes in, then one electron or a positron
respectively must leave, if an electron and a positron both go in, then their charges cancel so a zero
charge photon must leave, similarly if a photon goes in it must creates a electron and positron
pair.There are other more complex ways in which ingoing and outgoing particles can balance charge,
but as we'll see, all of these can be built up from basic one vertex diagram.

The another important rule is that overall interaction described by set of Feynman diagrams is
defined by the particles going in and the particles going out, these are the particles do we actually
measure, we know their properties, for example, their energy and momentum and they obey
Einstein's mass energy equation. We say that these particles are on the mass shell or just “on Shell”.
They sit on the shell structure you get when plot Einstein's equation of energy, momentum and mass.
On the other hand, everything that happens between the ingoing and outgoing tracks has questionable
reality. Each possible diagram that results in the same ingoing and outgoing particles is a valid
part of the possibility space for that interaction. The particles that have their entire existence
between vertices within the diagram, but don't enter all leave, are called virtual particles. They are
also by definition unmeasurable, otherwise they'd be one of our ingoing or outgoing particles. These
particles do not obey mass energy equivalence, so they are “off shell”. These particles aren't even
limited by the speed of light or the direction of time, which leads to all sorts of fun. [Ref. 1]

Part of the beauty of Feynman diagrams is that each of these diagrams themselves represents an
infinite number of specific interactions to start with, each of the particle paths are actually
infinite paths as well as infinite possibilities for particle momenta. We have to consider cases
even faster than speed of light paths which is really important for any particle besides the ingoing
and outgoing “on shell” particles any energy, speed and even direction in time is possible.

For example, for two electrons exchanging a single photon, it doesn't matter if we draw the
photon go from the first to the second or the second to the first. Even though this seems like a
very different interaction, we can think of the differences just being the photon traveling forward

In multiplicative renormalization, we calculate 1 vertex Feynman amplitudes in perturbation
theory until we encounter a divergent graph. The amplitude is then regularized with a cut off
momentum. The regularized amplitude is then expanded as Taylor series about zero external
momenta (for massive theories) to separate out the local divergent parts. We then add counter
terms to the Lagrangian density to exactly cancel all the divergences. This method makes theory
finite.

                                         CONCLUSION
The equations of quantum field theory allow us to calculate the behavior of subatomic particles
by expressing them as vibrations in quantum fields. In this project we saw that how Feynman
rules are used to observe different types of scattering with their most probable Feynman
diagrams and to calculate their S matrix. After that, regularization method is used to resolve the
divergences that appear in the Feynman diagrams which, in turn, gives them a senseful meaning.
We further determine that which of the Feynman diagram in a theory will be divergent (without
actually evaluating the integrals) using the notion of the superficial degree of divergence of a
graph in theory of Renormalization.
